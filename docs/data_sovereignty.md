# Deciding together who governs Igluligaarjuk's microbial DNA

No single law gives the Inuit of Igluligaarjuk (Chesterfield Inlet) legal ownership of the microbes in their waters, the genomes rebuilt from them, or the Inuktitut names chosen for them. Canada is **not a Party to the Nagoya Protocol** and has **no national access and benefit-sharing (ABS) system**, and the Nunavut Agreement has no genetic-resources article. Instead, the project runs on three layers. The first is a binding Nunavut permit layer, already in place for this project: an NRI Scientific Research Licence and university ethics approval. They bring ongoing duties: translated annual reports, publications sent to NRI, results returned to the community, and an amendment before any new use of samples. The second is Inuit-authored "soft law" that explicitly reaches environmental data: ITK's *National Inuit Strategy on Research* (Priority 4) and ICC's *Circumpolar Inuit Protocols*, whose Protocol 7 covers "all raw … scientific information and data collected within Inuit Nunaat". The third is contracts and ethics rules (TCPS 2 Chapter 9, the Tri-Agency data policy, NSERC's new engagement template), which make a written community agreement the main tool for deciding who controls what. The sharpest practical tension is that a SeqCode name is valid only if its type genome is **publicly available in INSDC**, and INSDC allows **no use restrictions** and no recall. The community's real control therefore sits at the moment *before* deposit, and afterwards in what stays outside the public record: the physical samples, other genomes, precise locations, the names' stories, and the Local Contexts labels that travel with the data. Monetary benefit-sharing under the new UN mechanism for digital sequence information is decoupled from source communities and nearly empty (about US$9,000 in late October 2026), so the benefits that count here are negotiated up front: credit, co-authorship, training, payment for knowledge, and returning results in Inuktitut. As far as the research found, Igluligaarjuk would be the first case anywhere of community members themselves choosing Inuktitut names for prokaryotes under the SeqCode, which makes getting the governance right a precedent, not just a formality.

## 1. Conceptual frameworks: Inuit-authored principles already cover environmental DNA

### Indigenous data sovereignty began as a claim about power, not about privacy

Indigenous data sovereignty (IDSov) is the right of Indigenous Peoples to govern the collection, ownership and use of data about their peoples, lands, waters and resources. The field took shape in national networks around 2015–2018: Te Mana Raraunga in Aotearoa, Maiam nayri Wingara in Australia, the US Indigenous Data Sovereignty Network, and others. Its foundational text is Kukutai and Taylor's 2016 edited volume, which frames data "about" Indigenous peoples as a site of colonial power and grounds Indigenous data rights in UNDRIP ([ANU Press](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2140/pdf/book.pdf)) [U]. Te Mana Raraunga's definition of Māori data includes data about "the environments we have relationships with" ([Te Mana Raraunga](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz)) [U]. That is an early, explicit precedent for counting environmental data as Indigenous data even when no person is in it.

The best-known product is the **CARE Principles** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics), released by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in 2019 and published in *Data Science Journal* in 2020 ([GIDA](https://www.gida-global.org/care); [Carroll et al. 2020, doi:10.5334/dsj-2020-043](https://doi.org/10.5334/dsj-2020-043)) [U for exact sub-principle wording]. CARE was designed to sit beside, not replace, the **FAIR** principles of open science: FAIR is about the data, CARE is about the people and purpose ("Be FAIR and CARE"). One CARE sub-principle (R3, under Responsibility) explicitly names "Indigenous languages and worldviews", which makes it the natural bridge to a naming workshop [U]. CARE has also moved into binding-adjacent international text: CBD Decision 16/2 asks public sequence databases to follow data governance consistent with "FAIR, **CARE** and TRUST" principles ([CBD Decision 16/2, para 10(d)](https://www.cbd.int/doc/decisions/cop-16/cop-16-dec-02-en.pdf)) [V]. Carroll et al. 2021 showed how to make CARE machine-actionable through provenance, permissions and protocols in metadata, which is where Local Contexts labels come from ([doi:10.1038/s41597-021-00892-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-021-00892-0)) [U].

### OCAP® is a First Nations framework; Inuit have their own

**OCAP®** (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) is often used loosely in Canada for "Indigenous data". It is a **First Nations** framework, a registered trademark of the First Nations Information Governance Centre, rooted in the 1998 First Nations Regional Health Survey ([FNIGC](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/)) [U; fnigc.ca was unreachable]. Canada's UN Declaration Act Action Plan commits to *distinctions-based* Indigenous data strategies, meaning separate First Nations, Inuit and Métis approaches ([CSPS](https://www.csps-efpc.gc.ca/tools/articles/indigenous-data-sovereignty-eng.aspx)) [S]. The Tri-Agency data policy likewise names OCAP® as "one First Nations model" and requires a distinctions-based approach ([science.gc.ca](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy)) [V]. For an Inuit audience, OCAP® belongs on a slide only as a labelled comparison. The Inuit-authored equivalents below use overlapping language ("access, ownership and control") but come from Inuit institutions.

### ITK's research strategy reaches wildlife and environment data

The anchor Inuit document is Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami's **National Inuit Strategy on Research (NISR)**, launched 22 March 2018. **Priority Area 4 is "Ensure Inuit access, ownership, and control over data and information"** ([ITK NISR PDF](https://www.itk.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/ITK-National-Inuit-Strategy-on-Research.pdf)) [V]. ITK frames self-determination in research as "determining how data and information about our people, **wildlife, and environment** is collected, stored, used, and shared" ([ITK NISR page](https://itk.ca/projects/national-inuit-strategy-on-research/)) [S]. Its objectives include "ownership of Inuit data by Inuit-appointed entities" and "Utilization of Inuktut … in data platforms and information management", and Action 4.3 calls for Inuit-specific guidelines on data accessibility, ownership and control ([ITK NISR PDF](https://www.itk.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/ITK-National-Inuit-Strategy-on-Research.pdf)) [V]. The other four priorities are governance, ethical conduct, funding alignment and capacity-building [S]. NISR is guidance with "no teeth or enforcement mechanisms" ([Nunatsiaq News](https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/65674the_national_inuit_research_strategy_five_things_you_need_to_know/)) [S]. But NRI asks every licence applicant to read it ([NRI Guide, May 2025](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V], so in Nunavut it works as the expected standard.

The follow-on **National Inuit Data Strategy** is **not yet published**. Budget 2021 funded it (reportedly $4M over three years) [S]. ICC Canada's 2025–26 Annual Report records a February 2026 National Inuit Data Management Committee meeting in Halifax with "substantial progress made … toward a published national Inuit Data Strategy" ([ICC Canada 2025–26 Annual Report](https://www.inuitcircumpolar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026-AnnualReport-Digital-ENG-Final.pdf)) [V]. Nunavut Tunngavik Inc. (NTI) is separately developing a **Nunavut Data Strategy**. Its stated principles are Inuit control of data management, community access to data "about them and from their communities", handling in line with Inuit values, and data "used for purposes and shared with parties only that Inuit agree with" ([Arctic Focus](https://arcticfocus.org/stories/upholding-inuit-data-sovereignty-nunavut)) [V, secondary]. Neither strategy should be quoted as if its content were known. Neither ITK nor NTI has issued a statement specifically on environmental genomic or microbial data (negative search result).

### ICC Protocol 7 is the most directly applicable Inuit text for outside researchers

The Inuit Circumpolar Council's ***Circumpolar Inuit Protocols for Equitable and Ethical Engagement*** (June 2022) were developed over about three years with delegates from Alaska, Canada, Greenland and Chukotka ([ICC PDF](https://www.inuitcircumpolar.com/wp-content/uploads/EEE-Protocols-LR-WEB.pdf); [UNFCCC mirror](https://lcipp.unfccc.int/sites/default/files/2025-06/EEE-Protocols-LR-WEB.pdf)) [V]. There are eight protocols, among them "Nothing About Us Without Us", "Recognize Indigenous Knowledge in its Own Right", "Communicate with Intent", and **Protocol 7, "Information and Data Sharing, Ownership, and Permissions"**. Protocol 7 states that "Inuit need **access, ownership and control over information, data and materials** pertaining to our knowledge, people, culture, **resources and homelands**" and that "Inuit access to **all raw information and data collected within Inuit Nunaat** should be agreed upon and ensured. **Including scientific information and data.**" Its directives include involving Inuit in analysis and interpretation, "Give credit where credit is due", "Require Inuit review and agreement on all materials and products … prior to public release", and "Report back in appropriate and accessible language" (pp. 26–28) [V]. Protocol 1 defines free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) as including "the ability to say 'yes', 'yes with conditions', and 'no'" and warns that "**silence does not mean agreement or yes**" (p. 17) [V]. Protocol 2 cautions that Indigenous Knowledge "must not be translated, integrated into, or validated by science" (p. 18) [V]. Of all the texts found, "materials pertaining to our … resources and homelands" and "raw … scientific information and data" make the clearest Inuit-authored case that water samples, DNA, reads and genomes from Igluligaarjuk fall under Inuit permission expectations.

### Non-human genomes are now treated as Indigenous data too

A largely Māori- and Native American-led literature argues that genomes of non-human organisms from Indigenous lands and waters carry Indigenous rights. Kukutai and Black (*Science*, 2024) argue that non-human genomic data deserve to be held "in similar esteem" to human genomic data. They use the little bush moa genome as their example and describe its deposit in the **Aotearoa Genomic Data Repository (AGDR)**, where access is governed under CARE ([Waikato PDF](https://www.waikato.ac.nz/assets/Uploads/Research/Research-institutes-centres-and-groups/Institutes/TKRI/Publications/Science_Kukutai2024_Careing_Indigenous_Nonhuman_Genomic_Data.pdf)) [S]. The *Te Nohonga Kaitiaki* guidelines for genomic research on taonga species (2016; a 2021 edition lists microbiologist Matthew Stott among its authors) give a Māori process for exactly this kind of work ([2021 PDF](https://sites.massey.ac.nz/teaurangahau/wp-content/uploads/sites/53/2021/10/TNKGuidelines.pdf)) [V]. Hudson et al. 2020 argue that unrestricted open access to genomic data, human and non-human, can conflict with Indigenous rights ([doi:10.1038/s41576-020-0228-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-020-0228-x)) [U]. Mc Cartney et al. 2022 make the same case for efforts to sequence all of life ([doi:10.1073/pnas.2115860119](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2115860119)) [U]. Dhami et al. 2025 note that DNA from many taonga species has gone into public repositories without restriction ([doi:10.1111/1755-0998.70021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1755-0998.70021)) [S]. In microbiology, *Nature Microbiology* published a relational framework for microbiome research with Indigenous communities in 2023 ([doi:10.1038/s41564-023-01471-2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-023-01471-2)) [S]. That framework and the companion "microbiome ownership" perspective are mostly about the human gut. **No Inuit-authored statement on microbial genetic resources and no Inuit-led environmental genome project with a published data agreement were found.** An honest framing for the workshop is that microbes are not "taonga" in an Inuit framework by default. Whether particular waters or organisms carry cultural significance is for the community to say.

### Words are data: Inuktitut names need their own governance

Names are language data, and language data has its own sovereignty tradition. Te Hiku Media's **Kaitiakitanga Licence** governs Māori speech data so that it is used only for Māori benefit, with Te Hiku acting as kaitiaki (guardian) ([ITU 2022](https://www.itu.int/hub/2022/08/ai-indigenous-languages-maori-te-reo/); licence repository at github.com/TeHikuMedia/Kaitiakitanga-License) [S; licence text not read]. UNDRIP Article 13 recognises the right to revitalise and transmit languages and writing systems and "to designate and retain their own names" ([UNDRIP](https://www.un.org/development/desa/indigenouspeoples/declaration-on-the-rights-of-indigenous-peoples.html)) [U for exact wording]. A validly published SeqCode name cannot be withdrawn. Classification is not fixed: as taxonomists redraw group boundaries, a microbe may be moved to another genus or merged with another species and take a different name, but the original name stays in the nomenclatural record (as a synonym) with its etymology and authors. FPIC on the word, its meaning, its spelling and how it is credited therefore matters more for a name than for almost any other workshop output.

### Three tensions the workshop should name openly

The first tension is **open science versus sovereignty**. Hudson, Mc Cartney and colleagues propose "as open as possible, as closed as necessary", with provenance and permission metadata rather than blanket closure [U], while the AGDR shows that controlled access for non-human genomes is technically possible [V]. The second is **helicopter research**. ICC wrote its protocols in response to long frustration with researchers who engaged too little, and Protocol 5 criticises leaving "without sharing the data that has been collected" ([ICC PDF](https://www.inuitcircumpolar.com/wp-content/uploads/EEE-Protocols-LR-WEB.pdf)) [V]. The third is **whose microbes these are**. Marine microbes disperse, the same taxa likely occur elsewhere, and a genome assembled from metagenomes (a MAG) is a reconstruction built with researchers' labour. No source resolves this. A defensible position is **shared stewardship with Inuit authority over local samples, metadata, names and benefits**, rather than a claim that anyone "owns" a species.

## 2. Legal frameworks: binding rules are thin, so agreements carry the weight

### International: strong norms, little direct force on a Canadian lab

**UNDRIP** (UNGA res. 61/295, 2007) is the rights backbone that CARE, NISR and ICC Protocol 1 all cite. Its **Article 31** covers Indigenous control over cultural heritage and traditional knowledge, "including human and **genetic resources**, seeds, medicines, knowledge of the properties of fauna and flora", and the related intellectual property. Article 13 covers languages and names, and Articles 18–19 cover participation and FPIC ([UNDRIP text](https://www.un.org/development/desa/indigenouspeoples/wp-content/uploads/sites/19/2018/11/UNDRIP_E_web.pdf)) [U for exact wording; copy from the UN page before quoting]. Article 31's explicit mention of "genetic resources" is the usual conceptual hook for Indigenous interests in non-human DNA.

The **Convention on Biological Diversity** binds Canada as a state. Article 15 gives states sovereign rights over genetic resources, with access subject to prior informed consent and mutually agreed terms. Article 8(j) commits Parties to respect Indigenous knowledge and share its benefits ([CBD text](https://www.cbd.int/convention/text)) [U for article wording]. Canada, however, has "no single, comprehensive access and benefit-sharing (ABS) system" ([ECCC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/corporate/international-affairs/partnerships-organizations/nagoya-protocol-access-genetic-resources.html)) [V, page dated Sept 2022]. In practice the CBD imposes no direct duty on a university researcher sampling in Nunavut. The **Nagoya Protocol**, with its community PIC, mutually agreed terms and respect for community protocols (Arts. 5, 6(2), 7, 12), "is not legally binding on Canada" because Canada has not ratified it ([ECCC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/corporate/international-affairs/partnerships-organizations/nagoya-protocol-access-genetic-resources.html)) [V; no accession found up to Oct 2026]. In 2012 the federal government cited a "lack of preparedness", and Indigenous peoples objected that they had not been consulted ([ABS Canada](https://abs-canada.org/leader-turned-laggard-canada-the-nagoya-protocol-and-the-road-ahead)) [S]. The Protocol is still the agreed global benchmark. If a journal or an EU collaborator asks for a "Nagoya statement", the accurate answer is that the samples were collected in Canada, Canada is not a Party, and collection took place under Nunavut licence no. … with community consent.

**Digital sequence information (DSI).** At COP16, Decision 16/2 (1 Nov 2024) created the **Cali Fund**, a "decoupled" multilateral mechanism. Large commercial users of DSI "should contribute … 1 per cent of their profits or 0.1 per cent of their revenue", and "public databases and public research and academic institutions are **not expected to make monetary contributions**" (para 9). All users "should share non-monetary benefits", including capacity-building for the self-identified needs of Indigenous peoples and local communities (paras 6–7). Databases "should" require country-of-origin information and an indication of traditional knowledge use (para 10). "At least half" of the funding should support Indigenous peoples and local communities (para 21), including those in **developed countries "where appropriate"** (para 18) ([Decision 16/2](https://www.cbd.int/doc/decisions/cop-16/cop-16-dec-02-en.pdf)) [V]. Uptake is tiny. The fund held **only about US$6,000** by mid-October 2026 ([Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop17-developed-countries-fall-far-short-of-un-targets-for-nature-finance)) [S] and **US$8,990** by 26 October per Earth Negotiations Bulletin reporting ([The Climate Watch](https://theclimatewatch.com/cop17-finance-divisions-persist-as-cali-fund-holds-just-8990/)) [S; totals conflict]. As of 26 October, COP17 had not resolved which commercial users must contribute or how the new permanent Article 8(j) body will work ([CBD press release](https://www.cbd.int/doc/press/2026/pr-2026-10-26-cop17-en.pdf)) [V]. **Final COP17 decisions are pending (the meeting closes 30 October 2026).** One consequence matters for the community: **once sequences are public, any downstream commercial user's obligations run to a global fund, not to Igluligaarjuk.** Community-specific benefits must be negotiated before deposit or delivered in non-monetary form.

The **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework** (Decision 15/4, 2022) is non-binding. Canada joined the consensus on Target 13 (benefit-sharing, including from DSI), Target 21 (traditional knowledge accessed only with FPIC) and Target 22 (full participation of Indigenous peoples and respect for their rights over lands, resources and knowledge) ([KMGBF](https://www.cbd.int/doc/decisions/cop-15/cop-15-dec-04-en.pdf)) [U for target wording]. The **WIPO GRATK Treaty** (May 2024) would require patent applicants to disclose the origin of genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge. It is **not in force** (4 of the 15 ratifications needed as of 8 July 2026), and **Canada did not sign** ([WIPO status](https://www.wipo.int/export/sites/www/treaties/en/docs/pdf/gratk.pdf)) [V]. The **BBNJ (High Seas) Agreement** entered into force on 17 January 2026 and covers microbial marine genetic resources, but only in areas beyond national jurisdiction, so it does **not** apply to Hudson Bay ([UN Geneva](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/news/2025/09/110787/un-high-seas-treaty-clears-ratification-threshold-enter-force)) [S]. It is still useful as a model of provenance identifiers plus FPIC for Indigenous knowledge. Finally, the **INSDC** databases (NCBI, ENA, DDBJ) guarantee "free and unrestricted access with **no use restrictions**" and permanent availability ([INSDC policy](https://www.insdc.org/?p=31)) [V]. They require country and collection date for new BioSamples ([INSDC](https://insdc.org/?p=309)) [V], and the `/country` field was renamed `/geo_loc_name` in 2024 ([DDBJ](https://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp/news/en/2024-02-01-e.html)) [V].

### Canada and Nunavut: the licence is the law that bites

The ***UN Declaration Act*** (S.C. 2021, c. 14) and its 2023–2028 Action Plan (181 measures) bind the federal government, not researchers ([Justice Canada](https://justice.gc.ca/eng/declaration/ap-pa/guide/index.html)) [V]. A Shared Priorities measure commits to work with Indigenous peoples on IP protection for traditional knowledge ([IPIC](https://ipic.ca/news/federal-government-issues-action-plan-for-implementation-of-the-united-nations-declaration-on-the-rights-of-indigenous-peoples-act-2023-06-22)) [S; measure number unverified]. The 2026 annual report lists Inuit Nunangat Policy guidance as "Underway" and has **no separate Inuit data measure** ([Justice Canada 2026, Annex B](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/declaration/report-rapport/2026/b3.html)) [V].

The **Nunavut Agreement** (1993) has no research or genetic-resources article; a legal analysis calls Nunavut's genetic-resources regime "rather inchoate" ([Cambridge chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E82D412D0E644936444E0B3FC25A2352/9781108470766c5_80-97.pdf/making_room_for_the_nagoya_protocol_in_nunavut.pdf)) [S]. It shapes sampling indirectly in three ways. **Article 21** says non-Inuit "may not enter, cross or remain on Inuit Owned Lands without the consent" of the Designated Inuit Organization, here the Kivalliq Inuit Association (KivIA) ([NTI presentation](https://www.tunngavik.com/documents/publications/administration/IOL%20and%20Minerals%2025Nov09.pdf)) [S]. KivIA's instrument is reportedly a "Certificate of Exemption" ([GN guide](https://prod.gov.nu.ca/sites/default/files/forms/2022-01/Guide_English.pdf)) [U]. **Articles 11–12** set up the planning and impact-screening system: NRI states that Nunavut Planning Commission (NPC) review "is required for ALL new land- and water-based research" and that NIRB screening is also needed unless NPC exempts the project ([NRI Guide](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V]. **Article 5** sets up wildlife management through the NWMB, which describes "wildlife" as mammals, birds, fish and plants ([NWMB FAQ](https://www.nwmb.com/en/about-nwmb/faqs)) [S]. Whether microbes count as "wildlife" is unsettled, but the practical reading is that microbial sampling falls under the Scientists Act, not the Wildlife Act. Marine waters off Chesterfield Inlet are generally Crown, not Inuit Owned Lands, but individual sampling sites must be checked against KivIA and NTI maps [U].

Nunavut's **Scientists Act** says "No person shall carry on scientific research in or based on Nunavut, or collect specimens in Nunavut for use in scientific research" without an NRI licence. "Limnology, water quality" and "oceanography" are listed as licensed topics ([NRI Guide, May 2025](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V]. The guide sets a minimum of **45 business days** of consultation with the Hamlet, the HTO, the District Education Authority, the Regional Inuit Association, NTI and others, any of whom can recommend rejection or add conditions. It also requires a **translated project summary and translated consent forms** before review starts. Annual Summary Reports are due by 31 December, and copies of final publications go to NRI. Crucially, "**you may not share research samples or specimens … outside the scope of your original licensed project. A license amendment or new research license must be obtained for any secondary research or analysis of archived samples**" [V]. Other authorizations on NRI's list include a Nunavut Water Board authorization "to collect water samples", Regional Inuit Association permission for Inuit Owned Lands, and prior notice to the Nunavut Film Development Corporation before filming people [V]. The guide exempts "visits to communities to disseminate research results" and early co-design engagement, but says licensing is "usually required" when academic partners will publish from what is gathered [V]. This project **already holds an NRI licence and university REB approval**, and community reports and results-return workshops are part of its plan. What remains is to confirm that the existing licence and REB scope cover publishing the community-chosen names (and any knowledge recorded with them) and any new uses of the samples.

Nunavut's ***Inuit Language Protection Act*** and ***Official Languages Act*** (both 2008) require organizations offering public-facing services in Nunavut to provide them in Inuktut ([Nunatsiaq News](https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/65674new_inuktut_language_requirements_come_into_force_for_nunavut_organiza/); [Languages Commissioner](https://langcom.nu.ca/sites/langcom.nu.ca/files/InuitLanguagePlanGuidelinesEN.pdf)) [S]. Neither Act regulates scientific names. The statutory terminology body, **Inuit Uqausinginnik Taiguusiliuqtiit (IUT)**, approves standard terms after consulting Elders ([IUT Annual Report](https://assembly.nu.ca/sites/default/files/TD%209-4%283%29%20ENG%20Inuit%20Uqausinginnik%20Taiguusiliuqtiit%20Annual%20Report%202013-2014.pdf)) [S]. It has no jurisdiction over Latin binomials but is a legitimate authority on spellings and root meanings.

**TCPS 2 (2022) Chapter 9** applies to the workshop, because people contribute knowledge and language. Pure environmental sampling is outside REB review, although Chapter 9 notes that community customs "may still require permissions and reporting" for natural-science work on Inuit lands. The key articles are 9.1 (engagement when research is on Inuit lands), 9.2 (the form of engagement is decided jointly), 9.13 ("The research should benefit the participating community"), 9.17 (community participation in interpretation before publication), 9.18 (IP "should be specified in a research agreement") and 9.20 (secondary use of data identifiable to an Indigenous community needs REB review) ([TCPS 2 Ch. 9](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html)) [V]. The **Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy** requires that data management plans for research "by and with" Inuit "must be co-developed" with communities, recognize Indigenous data sovereignty and allow renegotiation. It also says community direction "could result in **exceptions to the data deposit requirement**" ([science.gc.ca](https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/interagency-research-funding/policies-and-guidelines/research-data-management/tri-agency-research-data-management-policy)) [V]. NSERC's new **Indigenous Engagement and Collaboration Agreement Template (IECAT, CY2027)** goes further. The community partner signs it, and it requires researchers to "Respect and uphold Indigenous data sovereignty, including community control over ownership, access and future data use". It treats consent as ongoing and revisitable if "data or sample use" changes ([NSERC IECAT](https://nserc-crsng.canada.ca/sites/default/files/2026-06/IECAT-GAPCA_CY2027_e.docx)) [V].

On **property and IP**, no Canadian or Nunavut law gives anyone a property right in DNA sequences. Raw facts are not copyrightable, and SeqCode names are nomenclatural acts, not property. Individual Inuktitut words are not copyrightable, but recordings, written etymologies, artwork and handouts made at the workshop are copyright works belonging to their creators unless licensed (inference from Canadian IP principles; see the [Cambridge chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E82D412D0E644936444E0B3FC25A2352/9781108470766c5_80-97.pdf/making_room_for_the_nagoya_protocol_in_nunavut.pdf)) [U]. Control is therefore **contractual and ethical**, which is why the agreement is the central tool. On **privacy**, participants' personal information is governed by the university's home-province public-sector privacy law (for the University of Manitoba, Manitoba's statute) and TCPS 2 Chapter 5. Nunavut's ATIPP binds Nunavut public bodies such as the Hamlet and NRI [U]. Naming an individual in a protologue is a disclosure that needs explicit, revocable-before-publication consent.

## 3. The pragmatic side: what this means at the workshop table

### Licence and ethics approval are already in place; what they commit the project to

The project **already holds an NRI Scientific Research Licence and university research ethics (REB) approval**, and community reports and results-return workshops are built into its plan. The licence and approval are therefore not to-dos. They are a set of **ongoing obligations**, and the community should know about them, because they are also the community's levers. Under the NRI guide ([NRI Guide](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V], the licence commits the project to five things. It must file an **Annual Summary Report** by 31 December, translated, and keep doing so while analysis and publication continue, because genome-building and writing up are still "research based on Nunavut". It must send NRI **copies of final publications and theses**. It must share results directly with Nunavut agencies and community members and involve them in interpreting findings. It must get a **licence amendment before any change in sample use**: no sharing of samples or analysis of archived samples outside the licensed scope. And it must provide **translated** (Inuktut) summaries and consent materials. The REB approval commits the project to the consent procedures, translated forms and confidentiality terms it approved. Changes to those, such as adding recordings or naming individuals, need an REB amendment. The related field permissions for the 2025 sampling (NPC review with NIRB screening or an NPC exemption, any Nunavut Water Board authorization, and KivIA consent if any site was on Inuit Owned Lands) belong in the project file, so the community can see them. One scope check remains: **confirm in writing that the existing licence and REB approval cover publishing community-chosen names and etymologies (with any Inuit knowledge recorded alongside them) and any new uses of samples or genomes** (for example, re-binning for other questions or sharing extracts with collaborators). If they do not, amend before the names are registered. The Peru case, where work expanded beyond its authorized scope into gut-microbiome profiling, shows why the sample-use part matters ([News-Medical summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20231027/Ethical-microbiome-research-with-Indigenous-communities-A-framework-for-collaboration-and-sovereignty.aspx)) [S]. Licence reviewers in Nunavut are mostly unpaid. Only 35% are paid for their time, and they asked for "more direct engagement with researchers and better access to research outcomes" ([Beltrano et al. 2025, *Arctic*](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/view/83527)) [V]. Presenting in person to the Hamlet, the Aqigiq HTO and the DEA, and paying for that meeting time, directly answers what reviewers asked for.

### Consent at a workshop: layered, translated, and never inferred from silence

Consent should work at two levels. At the **community level**, written support is needed from the bodies NRI consults: the Hamlet council, the **Aqigiq Hunters and Trappers Organization** (name per [Legislative Assembly correspondence](https://assembly.nu.ca/node/4407)) [S], and the DEA where youth are involved. Ideally there is also an agreement with the project's knowledge-keeper partner, the **Sapujiyiit Society** ([Nunatsiaq News, 7 Sep 2026](https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/researchers-study-oil-eating-microbes-off-hudson-bay-coast/)) [V]. At the **individual level**, the REB-approved, translated opt-in consent (check it covers these; additions need an REB amendment) (syllabics, Roman orthography and English, read aloud by an interpreter) should have separate choices for: (a) taking part; (b) being photographed; (c) audio or video recording; (d) being named as a proposer of a scientific name or in publications; (e) being quoted; and (f) individual versus community-only credit. Minors need guardian consent through Victor Sammurtok School and the DEA. Filming needs prior notice to the Nunavut Film Development Corporation ([NRI Guide](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V]. The ICC standard should govern the form of consent: "yes", "yes with conditions" and "no" are all valid answers, and **silence is not agreement** ([ICC Protocol 1](https://www.inuitcircumpolar.com/wp-content/uploads/EEE-Protocols-LR-WEB.pdf)) [V]. The GN's honoraria directive notes that Elders "are identified by their community and will rarely identify themselves as such", so the Hamlet or HTO, not the research team, should identify Elder participants ([GN FAM 810-1](https://www.gov.nu.ca/sites/default/files/documents/2022-12/fam_810-1_honoraria_for_invited_event_participants_to_be_posted_nov_24_2022.pdf)) [V]. Consent to a name should stay open until the moment of registration, and the consent form should state plainly that **after valid publication a name cannot practically be withdrawn**.

### Agreements: one short document that covers samples, genomes and names

TCPS 2 Article 9.18, the Tri-Agency data policy and NSERC's IECAT all point to a written research agreement. No published Inuit-community research agreement exists for Nunavut, so the agreement has to be assembled from tested clauses (Section 4). It should cover: ownership or stewardship of physical samples and DNA extracts, and their storage, return or destruction; which data go public, when, and with what metadata; the community's standing copy of all data and results; a pre-release review window; rules for new uses (fresh consent, consistent with NRI's secondary-use rule); commercialization (notice, renegotiation, benefit-sharing); credit and co-authorship; honoraria; reporting back in Inuktut; and dispute resolution. The most important drafting lesson is about deposition. Most templates, including Memorial University's and the NWT CIMP template, promise data return, cessation of use or destruction "upon written request". **That promise cannot be kept for anything deposited in INSDC.** The agreement therefore needs an explicit, consented carve-out for type-genome deposition, modelled on the Kākāpō125+ terms, which allow deposit "as journals require" while banning onward transfer and unauthorised commercial use of everything else ([DOC NZ](https://www.doc.govt.nz/our-work/kakapo-recovery/what-we-do/research-for-the-future/kakapo125-gene-sequencing/request-kakapo125-data/)) [V].

### Data deposition versus control: the SeqCode makes one thing public and leaves the rest negotiable

SeqCode **Rule 18a** reads: "The sequence must be available in the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC)". **Rule 26** makes valid publication depend on registration with a designated type whose sequence "is deposited according to Rule 18a and the accession number cited" ([SeqCode, v1.2.0](https://registry.seqco.de/page/seqcode)) [V]. The Registry flags non-INSDC type sequences as "Unrecognized type material" ([example record](https://registry.seqco.de/seqcode/names/25894)) [V]. The Code says nothing about embargo, controlled access or Indigenous data. Two consequences follow. A type genome held only in a controlled-access repository such as the AGDR or NativeBio **cannot carry a valid SeqCode name**, and permanent embargo has the same effect. But the SeqCode requires *sequence* deposition, not culture or sample deposition, so a good deal stays negotiable. That includes the physical samples and extracts (**the strongest sovereignty lever**), the non-type genomes, fine-scale coordinates (which can be coarsened to the inlet), the stories and knowledge behind the names, and the timing of release through hold-until-publication dates. One open question changes this list. Secondary sources say the Registry also requires raw reads in SRA, while another reading treats raw-data access as an Appendix I recommendation ([ISME J 2024](https://academic.oup.com/ismej/article/18/1/wrae109/7696152)) [S, **conflicting**]. **Do not tell the community that reads can be withheld until this is checked on registry.seqco.de.** The honest risk statement for handouts is that a free-living bacterium's genome from seawater contains no human genetic information. The real sensitivities are site location, possible commercial (bioprospecting) value, and who tells the story of the name. Human DNA fragments can appear in environmental metagenomes as bycatch, so removing human reads before deposit is prudent practice [inference]. The Tri-Agency exception for community-directed deposit decisions applies to everything *except* the type genome of a named taxon, and the data management plan should record that distinction.

### Local Contexts labels: provenance that travels, not a lock

Local Contexts **Biocultural (BC) Labels** are applied only by Indigenous communities. **Notices** (BC Notice, TK Notice, Open to Collaborate) are applied by researchers or institutions to flag Indigenous provenance where Labels are not yet in place, and they can be added at any stage, including after deposition ([Local Contexts BC Labels](https://localcontexts.org/labels/biocultural-labels/); [Liggins, Hudson & Anderson 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mec.15918)) [S]. Each Hub project has a persistent ID, and Labels are fetched live through the API. **The community can therefore change its Labels after the sequence is public** ([Local Contexts Projects PDF](https://localcontexts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Local-Contexts-Projects.pdf)) [V]. The BC Provenance Label template states that the community "retains the right to be named and associated with it into the future" ([BC Provenance](https://localcontexts.org/label/bc-provenance/)) [V]. The AGDR became a certified Local Contexts integration partner in May 2026 ([Local Contexts](https://localcontexts.org/aotearoa-genomic-data-repository-joins-local-contexts-as-one-of-our-first-certified-integration-partners/)) [V]. The MAGIC muskox genomics project, co-led by Nunavut HTOs, is developing Labels ([MAGIC summary](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/aina/MAGIC_report_summary_2025.pdf)) [S]. **No NCBI, ENA or DDBJ field for Local Contexts was found** (negative result, October 2026). The practical routes are: the Hub URL in the BioProject description and as a custom BioSample attribute (NCBI accepts custom attributes; [BioSample docs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/biosample/docs/)) [S]; DataCite's `rights` element with `rightsIdentifierScheme="Local Contexts"` for any DOI-minted dataset ([DataCite](https://support.datacite.org/docs/local-contexts-notices-and-labels)) [V]; ERGA's metadata guidance ([Zenodo 8088227](https://zenodo.org/records/8088227/files/Guidance%20Documentation%20for%20Implementing%20the%20Traditional%20Knowledge%20and%20Biocultural%20Labels%20in%20the%20European%20Reference%20Genome%20Atlas.pdf)) [V]; the paper's data-availability statement; and the SeqCode protologue. Labels express expectations and relationships. They **do not create legal rights and do not restrict access** to INSDC records, and the slides should say so.

### Naming and credit: community proposal, recorded etymology, and real authorship

SeqCode allows roots "from any language in use or extinct" and requires an etymology. **Recommendation 9(3)**, however, says "Languages other than Latin should be avoided when Latin equivalents exist", and Recommendation 9(6) bars names that are "deliberately contentious or abusive" ([SeqCode](https://registry.seqco.de/page/seqcode)) [V]. Recommendations are not rules, so a protologue should briefly state the cultural reason for choosing Inuktitut roots. The nearest precedent is ***Candidatus* Tariuqbacter / class Tariuqbacteria** (Vigneron, Vincent & Lovejoy 2023, *ISME Communications* 3:82), from Lake A on Ellesmere Island. Its acknowledgements read: "We express our thanks to **Larry Audlaluk** for discussions in Grise Fiord about this work and the naming in Inuktitut" ([doi:10.1038/s43705-023-00287-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43705-023-00287-9)) [V, user-confirmed]. That makes it a real Inuktitut-rooted prokaryote name chosen by researchers in consultation with an Inuk Elder. The gloss "tariuq = salt water/sea" still needs confirming from the protologue [U], and no SeqCode registration was found [U]. Igluligaarjuk differs in kind, because community members themselves propose the names. The two cases set out a useful choice between **individual acknowledgement** and **community credit**. Elsewhere in taxonomy, the debate over eponyms and Indigenous names is active ([Guedes et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-023-02022-y.pdf)) [V], and Gillman and Wright propose restoring Indigenous names ([doi:10.1038/s42003-020-01344-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-020-01344-y)) [U]. *Flavobacterium akiainvivens* carries a Hawaiian common name alongside its Latin binomial ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavobacterium_akiainvivens)) [S, tertiary]. That suggests a model of a **parallel Inuktitut common name in syllabics** that keeps meaning Latinization cannot. For each name, the record should hold: the root in syllabics and Roman orthography; the meaning; the dialect (Aivilingmiutut is the western Hudson Bay dialect, though Tusaalanga lists Igluligaarjuk separately, so local forms may differ: [Tusaalanga](https://tusaalanga.ca/node/2515)) [S]; who proposed it, if they consented to be named; a statement such as "proposed by participants of the Igluligaarjuk community naming workshop, October 2026, with the Sapujiyiit Society"; the date of community approval; and the Local Contexts Hub URL. Under the SeqCode the name's authority is the authors of the effective publication, so **community members listed as co-authors become part of the permanent author citation** [inference]. That is the most durable form of credit available. The place-name system offers an Inuit model for approval: IHT and the GN Toponymy Program document Elder-endorsed forms and send them for formal approval ([GN Toponymy](https://www.gov.nu.ca/en/department-culture-and-heritage/toponymy-program)) [S]. Sending final roots to IUT for information is a gesture of respect, not a legal requirement.

### Benefit sharing, honoraria and returning results: negotiate first, pay properly, come back

Monetary benefit-sharing from public DSI flows through the Cali Fund, not to source communities, and the fund is nearly empty. Real precedents for community-specific deals are bilateral and negotiated *before* any value appears. In Nunavut, the UPEI Marine Natural Products Lab and NTI negotiated a "potentially precedent setting" ABS agreement for microbial bioprospecting ([PMC3754551](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3754551)) [S]. Greenland's Act No. 3 of 2016 requires a permit and a benefit-sharing agreement for genetic resources explicitly including **microorganisms**, notification of publications, and attribution of Greenlandic origin, and lets the government take IP co-ownership ([FAOLEX](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/grl201579.pdf)) [V, translation]. The Hoodia and Rooibos deals in South Africa show percentage royalties and levies paid into community trusts ([Cultural Survival](https://cs.org/node/6542); [Engineering News](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/landmark-deal-to-give-khoi-and-san-benefit-sharing-levy-in-rooibos-2019-11-01)) [S]. For this project, the agreement should include a simple **commercialization clause**: if anyone in the research team or a licensee pursues a patent or product from these genomes, the community is notified and terms are negotiated first. The realistic benefits are non-monetary, and the San Code of Research Ethics warns against "exaggerated claims of the researcher's lack of resources" as an excuse for providing none ([San Code](https://www.globalcodeofconduct.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/San-Code-of-RESEARCH-Ethics-Booklet_English.pdf)) [V]. Those benefits are naming credit and co-authorship, paid local employment (for example, field technician roles), youth training in sampling and sequencing, and capacity the community keeps.

**Honoraria.** The Government of Nunavut's own benchmark (FAM Directive 810-1, effective 1 January 2023) is **$650 per full day / $325 per half day** for IQ knowledge holders, Elders and panellists, and **$400 / $200** for event attendees. The half-day rate is the minimum, and rates are reviewed every three years, so check for a 2026 update ([GN FAM 810-1](https://www.gov.nu.ca/sites/default/files/documents/2022-12/fam_810-1_honoraria_for_invited_event_participants_to_be_posted_nov_24_2022.pdf)) [V]. NRI calls compensating engagement participants "best practice" and its application form asks for pay rates ([NRI Guide](https://www.nri.nu.ca/sites/default/files/License%20Application%20Guidleines_May2025.pdf)) [V]. ICC asks that compensation be agreed before work begins [S]. Interpreters should be budgeted separately, and the honoraria should be settled with the Hamlet or HTO before the sessions.

**Returning results.** In Nunatsiavut, about **two-thirds of research projects approved from 2011 to 2021 never returned data** to the community ([Ortenzi et al., *Ambio*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11662125/)) [V]. NTI publicly criticised the 2007–08 Inuit Health Survey for poor reporting back ([Nunatsiaq News](https://nunatsiaq.com/?p=22014)) [S]. NRI expects results to be shared "directly with Nunavut agencies and community members", with community members involved "in reviewing, interpreting, and validating your findings" [V], and requires a translated Annual Summary Report [V per one note; [U] whether translation is mandatory in every case]. In practice that means: a plain-language Inuktitut and English summary; posters and radio updates; the final SeqCode Registry entries and accession numbers sent to the Hamlet and HTO; and a community-held copy of data and results, for example with the HTO or on an Inuit-governed platform such as SIKU, whose terms say users own their content ([SIKU terms](https://about.siku.org/terms)) [S].

## 5. A proposed "who decides what" matrix, rebuilt around the release gate

The user's draft matrix had one core idea: an explicit who-decides-what for each kind of data. That idea is kept. The draft also had a contradiction. It gave the HTO sole control of access to "microbial DNA sequences" while also marking public databases and third parties as able to access them. The redesign resolves this with a **release gate**. Every data type starts under community-governed stewardship. Moving anything into public archives is a named decision with named deciders, and once released, type genomes are irrevocably open. The community's continuing control then shifts to the samples, the unreleased data, the metadata and Labels, and the names' stories. The roles are rebuilt as follows. **Contributors** are the individual participants, including Elders, youth and the field technician. The **Community governance group** is a body the community itself constitutes. The research team should not presume its membership, but candidates include the Hamlet, the Aqigiq HTO, the Sapujiyiit Society, a youth representative and, for minors, the DEA and parents. **Regional and territorial authorities** are KivIA, NTI, NRI, NPC/NIRB and the NWB. The **Research team** is the University of Manitoba group. **Archives** are INSDC, the SeqCode Registry, the Local Contexts Hub and any community platform. **Third-party users** are everyone else.

## Implications for the slides and handouts

For **community members (youth, Elders, HTO)**, the core messages are short. *Your waters, your say*: Inuit organizations (ITK, ICC) already say that data from Inuit lands and waters, including scientific data, should be under Inuit access, ownership and control. *You can say yes, yes with conditions, or no, and silence is not yes.* *One thing must go public*: for a name to become official worldwide, that microbe's genome must be shared publicly, and it stays in the public library. You decide, genome by genome, before that happens. *Everything else can stay with us*: the water samples, the DNA, other microbes, exact locations, and the stories behind the names stay under the agreement. *A published name can't be withdrawn*, though scientists may later move and rename the microbe: choose the word, spelling and meaning carefully, and decide whether to be named personally or as "the people of Igluligaarjuk". *Labels travel with the data*: the community can attach and later change Local Contexts Labels that say where the DNA came from. *Benefits come from the agreement, not from the UN fund*: credit, co-authorship, paid roles, training and results returned in Inuktitut are the benefits that are real today.

For **researchers**, the messages are: the NRI licence and REB approval are in place, and they carry ongoing duties (translated annual reports, publications to NRI, returning results, and an amendment before any new sample use), so confirm that their scope covers publishing names; Nagoya does not apply, but CARE, ICC Protocol 7 and NISR Priority 4 set the standard reviewers and funders apply; build the agreement around a release gate with a per-genome community decision and a written carve-out for INSDC deposit; do not borrow "silence = consent" review clauses; budget honoraria at or above the GN's $650/$325 rates; record each name's syllabics, orthography, dialect, meaning and approval; and treat community co-authorship of names as the most durable credit the SeqCode offers. One slide should explicitly separate **OCAP® (First Nations)** from **Inuit frameworks**. Another should show the Tariuqbacter case (researcher-chosen with an Elder's input) beside Igluligaarjuk (community-chosen) as two models of credit.

## Conclusion

The research shows that formal law matters less than the order in which decisions are made. Because Canada has no ABS regime and INSDC allows no use restrictions, control over these microbes cannot be enforced after deposit. It is decided at the release gate, kept in the samples and unreleased data, and expressed in metadata and authorship. That reverses the usual researcher instinct to "deposit, then consult": here, consultation and community approval of each name and each type genome must come first, or the community's leverage is gone for good. It also means the governance agreement and the naming protocol written for this workshop could become the first worked example of how a community-chosen name under the SeqCode handles Indigenous data sovereignty. The SeqCode itself is silent on that question.

Two things are still moving, and the dossier should be refreshed when they settle: COP17's decisions on DSI and databases, closing 30 October 2026, and the long-awaited National Inuit Data Strategy. Neither is likely to change the core advice. The Cali Fund's decoupled design already shows that benefits for Igluligaarjuk will come from what is negotiated locally, not from multilateral mechanisms. An Inuit data strategy would most likely confirm NISR's and ICC's expectations rather than loosen them. The strongest test of the workshop will be whether the names, data and results come back to the community in Inuktitut, the step that two-thirds of projects in the neighbouring Nunatsiavut region skipped.
