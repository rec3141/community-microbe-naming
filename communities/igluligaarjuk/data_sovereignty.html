Our waters, our say

We took water from the inlet and the lakes, read the DNA of the microbes in it, and are now giving some of them names.

Before we share anything, we want to agree together on who decides what. This is called data sovereignty.

Audience note: many people in the room have a grade 6–8 education. Keep to the slide words, use the interpreter, and give time for questions after each decision slide. This deck describes rules and what other communities have done. It is not legal advice. Before presenting, check the dossier's verification list (docs/data_sovereignty.md §6). Each step produces something different, and each can have its own rules about who keeps it and who can see it.

Also part of the "data": where and when we sampled, what people share at the workshop, photos and recordings, and papers and posters. A free-living bacterium's genome from seawater contains no human genetic information. The sensitive parts are where it came from, any money it could make, and who tells the story of its name.
The idea

What is data sovereignty?

Inuit decide how information about their people, land, water and animals is collected, kept, used and shared.

Scientists like to share

Scientists put their data online so others can check it and learn from it.

Communities have a say

Communities also want to know who agreed to the research, how it benefits them, and who looks after the data.

The two can work together: we share what we agree to share and look after the rest.

Indigenous data sovereignty grew from Indigenous networks worldwide from about 2015 (Kukutai & Taylor 2016, ANU Press). Other frameworks you may be asked about: CARE (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, worldwide) and OCAP® (a First Nations framework, not an Inuit one). The Inuit documents are on the next slide.
Inuit have already said this

What Inuit organizations have said about research data

Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (2018)

"Ensure Inuit access, ownership, and control over data and information."

This covers data about Inuit land, water and animals, not only about people.

Inuit Circumpolar Council (2022)

"Inuit access to all raw information and data collected within Inuit Nunaat should be agreed upon and ensured. Including scientific information and data."

It also asks that Inuit review results before they are made public.

ITK National Inuit Strategy on Research, Priority Area 4 [V]. ITK's own page describes it as data about "our people, wildlife, and environment" [S: confirm on itk.ca]. ICC Circumpolar Inuit Protocols for Equitable and Ethical Engagement, Protocol 7, pp. 26–28 [V]. The Nunavut Research Institute asks every licence applicant to read the ITK strategy. ITK's National Inuit Data Strategy and NTI's Nunavut Data Strategy are still being written (as of Feb 2026). Check for news before the workshop.
Saying yes or no

Three ways to answer

Yesgo ahead
Yes, with conditionsgo ahead, in the way agreed
Nodo not go ahead
We wait for a clear answer. Silence is not taken as a yes.

You can take part and still say no to photos, to recordings, or to having your own name in a paper.

ICC Protocol 1, p. 17 [V]: consent includes "the ability to say 'yes', 'yes with conditions', and 'no'"; "silence does not mean agreement or yes". Individual choices on the consent form: take part; photos; audio/video; named as a name's proposer; quoted. Check these match the REB-approved form; additions need an REB amendment. Youth need a parent's or guardian's consent. People can change their mind about a name until it is published. After that it can't be withdrawn, though taxonomists may later move the microbe and rename it as group boundaries are redrawn; the original name stays in the record as a synonym.

What the law says

The law and microbe DNA

What the laws cover

  • Canada has not joined the international treaty on sharing benefits from genetic resources (the Nagoya Protocol).
  • The Nunavut Agreement does not cover DNA.
  • No law says who owns microbe DNA.

What research rules say

  • Research with Inuit communities should benefit the community.
  • Ownership and use of data should be set out in a written agreement.
  • Our agreement with the HTO will do this.
Nagoya: ECCC says it "is not legally binding on Canada" [V; page dated Sept 2022, no later accession found]. Canada has no national access and benefit-sharing law [V]. UNDRIP Art. 31 names "genetic resources" as part of Indigenous heritage, but binds governments, not researchers [U wording]. TCPS 2 Chapter 9: Art. 9.13 (research should benefit the community) and 9.18 (intellectual property set out in a research agreement) [V]. Tri-Agency data policy: data plans for Inuit research "must be co-developed" with communities [V].
Already in place

What our research licence requires

This project has a licence from the Nunavut Research Institute and approval from the university's ethics board. Under these approvals:

Every year

  • We send a report, in Inuktitut and English.
  • We send copies of everything we publish.
  • We come back and share the results here.

Before anything new

  • The samples are not used for anything new without permission.
  • The samples are not shared outside the project.
NRI licence guide (May 2025) [V]: no sharing of samples "outside the scope of your original licensed project"; an amendment is needed "for any secondary research or analysis of archived samples". To confirm before the workshop (item 16): that the licence and REB approval cover publishing the names, their meanings and any knowledge recorded with them. Item 17: whether every annual report must be translated. Add the licence number here.
Making a name official

An official name comes with a public genome

The rules for naming microbes (the SeqCode) accept a name once that microbe's genome is shared in the world's public DNA library.

Open to all

Scientists anywhere can look at it.

Free to use

The library does not set rules on how it is used.

Permanent

It stays there for good.

For this reason, the community decides on each genome before it is shared.

SeqCode Rule 18a: the type sequence "must be available in the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC)"; Rule 26 makes valid publication depend on it [V, SeqCode v1.2.0]. INSDC: "free and unrestricted access with no use restrictions", permanent [V]. Open question (item 22): whether the Registry also requires the raw reads. Sources conflict, so don't promise anything about raw reads until it's checked on registry.seqco.de.
Sharing step by step

Deciding what is shared

Stays with us

  • Water samples and DNA
  • Genomes we are not naming
  • Exact sampling spots
  • The stories behind the names
🤝 Community review For each microbe we name, the community, led by the HTO, says yes, yes with conditions, or no.

Shared publicly

  • The genome of each named microbe
  • Its name and what the name means
  • Only the location details we agree to

The community reviews each genome and its name before it is shared.

Dossier §5 "release gate". A "no" means that microbe can't get an official SeqCode name; it can still have a community name. The water samples and DNA are the community's strongest control, because the naming rules only need the genome, not the samples. Model: the Kākāpō125+ genome terms (NZ Department of Conservation and Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu) allow deposit only "as journals require" and ban passing anything else on [V].
Others have done this

How other Indigenous groups manage DNA research

Nunavik

In their health survey, "Nunavik institutions own all data and biological samples." A regional committee approves every use.

Greenland

A 2016 law covers DNA from Greenland, including microbes. Researchers need a permit, must share benefits, and must say the DNA came from Greenland.

New Zealand

Māori guardians decide who can use the genomes of plants and animals that matter to them.

Nunavik: Ayotte et al. 2024 (PMC10830945), describing Qanuilirpitaa? 2017 governance [V]. Greenland Act No. 3 of 3 June 2016 (FAOLEX grl201579) [V, Danish original; the English here is a translation]. Aotearoa Genomic Data Repository: guardians (kaitiaki) approve access [V]; Kukutai & Black 2024, Science [S].
Labels

Labels that show where the DNA came from

An online label can be attached to the public genomes. It shows where the DNA came from and what the community expects.

What a label does

  • Names Igluligaarjuk as the place the DNA came from.
  • Only the community can choose its labels.
  • The community can change them later.

What a label does not do

  • It does not limit who can download the genome.
  • It states the community's expectations; it is not a legal rule.
Local Contexts Biocultural (BC) Labels are applied only by communities; researchers can add a BC Notice until then [S]. Labels are fetched live through the Hub API, so changes show up everywhere [V]. There's no dedicated NCBI/ENA field yet: put the Hub link in the BioProject description, a custom BioSample attribute, the paper's data statement and the protologue. Nunavut example: the MAGIC muskox genomics project, co-led by Nunavut HTOs [S].
Choosing names

What goes into a name

Each name will record

  • The word in syllabics and Roman letters
  • What it means
  • That it came from Igluligaarjuk
  • Who suggested it, if they want to be named

Once a name is published, we can't take it back. As scientists learn more, they may move a microbe to a new group and rename it, but the record of the first name, and who chose it, stays.

An earlier Inuktitut name

Tariuqbacter (2023) is a microbe from Ellesmere Island. Scientists picked the name and talked it over with an Elder in Grise Fiord, Larry Audlaluk.

In this workshop, community members choose the names. We have not found an earlier example of this.

Vigneron, Vincent & Lovejoy 2023, ISME Communications 3:82, doi:10.1038/s43705-023-00287-9. The acknowledgements thank Larry Audlaluk "for discussions in Grise Fiord about this work and the naming in Inuktitut" [V]. Check the protologue's gloss of "tariuq" before saying it aloud (item 25). SeqCode Rec. 9(3) prefers Latin roots, but recommendations aren't rules; each protologue should give the cultural reason for using Inuktitut roots. "Not done before" = no earlier community-chosen Inuktitut prokaryote names found in this research.
What the community gets

Benefits for the community

What the agreement will include

  • Credit for the community in every name
  • Pay for Elders, knowledge holders and interpreters
  • Paid local jobs and training for youth
  • Results brought back here, in Inuktitut
  • Anyone who wants to use these genomes commercially must ask the community first

The UN fund

In 2024 the UN set up a fund for companies that profit from public DNA data. It has collected less than $10,000 worldwide so far, and payments go to a global fund rather than to the places the DNA came from.

Cali Fund, CBD Decision 16/2 [V]; about US$6,000 to US$8,990 deposited by late Oct 2026 [S]. COP17 (Yerevan) closes 30 Oct 2026: update if anything changes. GN honoraria benchmark (FAM 810-1, from 1 Jan 2023): $650 a full day, $325 a half day for Elders and IQ knowledge holders [V]. Check for a 2026 update (item 30).
Returning results

Bringing results back to the community

A study in Nunatsiavut found that about two out of three research projects from 2011 to 2021 did not return their results to the community. Our plan:

How results will come back

  • A short report in Inuktitut and English, plus posters and radio.
  • The official names sent to the Hamlet and the HTO.
  • A copy of all the data kept here, in the community.
Ortenzi et al., Ambio (PMC11662125) [V]. Licence reviewers in Nunavut asked for "more direct engagement with researchers and better access to research outcomes" (Beltrano et al. 2025, Arctic) [V].

Decision 2 of 6

When does a genome go public?

OPTION A

Post all of them now

The fastest option; scientists can use them right away.

The community gives up its say before the names are chosen.

OPTION B

One at a time, after the name is approved

The data group sees each final name and says yes or no for that genome.

Takes longer.

OPTION C

Post none

Everything stays here.

No official names at all.

Why B: the names become official, and the community still decides each one.

📝 First draft: today's choices become final only when we sign a written agreement with the HTO.

This is the release gate (slide 10). Only genomes that carry a name need to go public. All other genomes stay under the agreement unless the data group decides otherwise. Data can be held privately until the paper or Registry entry is ready (NCBI hold-until-publication). Check the maximum hold period (item 23).
Decision 3 of 6

How exact should the locations be?

OPTION A

Exact GPS points

Most useful to scientists.

Shows exact spots, which may include private places.

OPTION B

The place name only

"Itivia" or "Puirsuk (1st Lake)". These are already on our website.

Less exact for scientists.

OPTION C

"Chesterfield Inlet" only

Shares the least detail.

Loses the difference between sea and lake.

Why B: it uses Inuktitut place names, shows nothing new, and still tells scientists whether a microbe lives in the sea or a lake.

📝 First draft: today's choices become final only when we sign a written agreement with the HTO.

The public DNA library requires country and collection date; exact coordinates are optional [V]. If any site is sensitive (a harvesting spot, a cabin lake), drop it, as docs/ethics-and-data.md says.
Decision 4 of 6

Who gets credit for each name?

OPTION A

Only the person who suggested it

Honours that person.

Leaves out everyone who helped.

OPTION B

Only "the people of Igluligaarjuk"

Everyone shares it.

People who want their own name on it can't have it.

OPTION C

Both

The community is always named. Each person can also be named, if they want to be.

One more question on the consent form.

Why C: the community always gets credit, and nobody is named who doesn't want to be.

📝 First draft: today's choices become final only when we sign a written agreement with the HTO.

Suggested wording for each name's record: "proposed by participants of the Igluligaarjuk community naming workshop, 2026", plus individual names only with opt-in consent. Community members listed as authors of the publication become part of the name's permanent citation; offer this as well.
Decision 5 of 6

What happens to the water samples and DNA?

OPTION A

Kept frozen at the university, owned by the community

They stay safe in the special freezers they need. Any new use needs the data group's permission.

They are far away.

OPTION B

Sent back to Igluligaarjuk

Physically here.

DNA spoils without a very cold freezer (−80 °C).

OPTION C

Destroyed when the project ends

Nobody can ever misuse them.

No future studies, even ones the community wants.

Why A: the samples stay usable for the community's future questions. The community can still ask for them to be destroyed at any time.

📝 First draft: today's choices become final only when we sign a written agreement with the HTO.

In the agreement: the community owns the samples; the university is the steward; no new use without the data group and an NRI licence amendment; destroy on request, with a letter confirming it. Models: Memorial University / Nunatsiavut agreement ("samples" included in Indigenous Data) [V]; Alberta FNIGC data-sharing framework (letter confirming destruction or return) [V].
